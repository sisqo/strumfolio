Most people learn a chord as a shape before they ever learn what it is. Three fingers there,
thumb behind the neck, and <Chord>G</Chord> becomes a hand position long before it becomes
anything else. That is a fine way to start playing, and a bad way to understand what happens
the first time a chart shows a chord your hand has never made.

**A chord is a set of notes.** The shape is just one way of reaching those notes on one
particular instrument — which means the shape is downstream of the notes, not the other way
around, and a diagram that is generated from the notes will always cover ground that a diagram
copied from a shape book cannot.

## What a chord name is actually saying

<Chord>C</Chord> names three notes: C, E and G. Add a number and you are adding a note by
interval — <Chord>C7</Chord> keeps those three and adds a B flat, a minor seventh above the
root. Change the quality and you are changing which of those intervals apply —
<Chord>Cm</Chord> lowers the E to an E flat, and the chord is minor because that one note moved.

Every symbol on a chart is built the same way, and it has exactly three parts:

- **The root**, which is the letter at the front. It names the note the chord is measured
  from, and it is the only part that changes when you [transpose](/blog/transposing-to-fit-your-voice).
- **The quality**, which is everything that decides major, minor, diminished or augmented. It
  is usually the smallest part of the symbol and always the most audible: `m` is one character
  and the difference between a chord that sounds bright and one that sounds sad.
- **The extensions**, the numbers and words after that — `7`, `sus4`, `add9`, `maj7` — each of
  which names one more note by its distance from the root.

**None of that mentions a fret, a string, or a finger.** It is a description of a sound, in the
same sense a recipe is a description of a dish rather than a description of a kitchen.

## One root, seven chords

The quickest way to feel how little of this is memorisation: hold the root still and change
only the intervals. Every chord below is built on C, and each differs from the one above it by
a single note.

- <Chord>C</Chord> — C, E, G. The root, a major third, a fifth.
- <Chord>Cm</Chord> — C, E flat, G. The third drops a semitone; that is the whole of minor.
- <Chord>C7</Chord> — C, E, G, B flat. A minor seventh on top of the major chord.
- <Chord>Cmaj7</Chord> — C, E, G, B. The same idea a semitone higher, and a completely
  different mood.
- <Chord>Csus4</Chord> — C, F, G. The third is *replaced*, not added, which is why a sus chord
  sounds unresolved: nothing in it says major or minor.
- <Chord>Cadd9</Chord> — C, E, G, D. A ninth added while the third stays.
- <Chord>Cdim</Chord> — C, E flat, G flat. Both the third and the fifth pulled down.

Seven chords, one letter, no shapes involved. **Once the notes are the thing you know, the
name of a chord you have never played is still readable** — and a chart that hands you
<Chord>Fadd9</Chord> at eleven o'clock at night is a chord you can work out rather than a chord
you are missing.

<Quote>The name is a recipe. The shape is one kitchen it can be cooked in.</Quote>

## From notes to a shape

The shape only enters once you ask a second question: on *this* instrument, where are those
notes? For a guitar that means finding each note of the chord somewhere on six strings, within
a hand span that can actually close around them — which is arithmetic on fret positions and
open strings, not a fact that has to be remembered separately for every chord.

That is the whole difference between looking a chord up and working it out. A lookup table
answers "what shape is <Chord>Dm7</Chord>" by having somebody type that shape in ahead of time.
**Working it out from the notes answers the same question by finding D, F, A and C on the
fretboard** — which also answers a question nobody typed in ahead of time, because it did not
need to be anticipated.

## Why one chord has several shapes

If a chord is a set of notes, then any arrangement of those notes is that chord — and a guitar
can reach most sets in four or five places. That is not ambiguity, it is choice:

- **A different position on the neck.** <Chord>C</Chord> as an open shape and <Chord>C</Chord>
  barred at the third fret are the same three notes in a different register.
- **A different order.** Put E at the bottom instead of C and you have an inversion: same
  chord, different colour, often a smoother move from whatever came before it.
- **A different number of notes.** Nothing says all six strings have to sound. Three notes are
  a full major chord; the rest is doubling.

Slash chords are this made explicit. <Chord>D/F#</Chord> is an ordinary D — D, F sharp, A —
with the F sharp put in the bass, and the reason it appears in a chart is almost always a bass
line walking down while the harmony stays put. **The note after the slash is usually already in
the chord**; when it is not, as in <Chord>C/D</Chord>, the chart is asking for a genuinely
different sound rather than a rearrangement.

## Where a lookup table runs out

A table is only as complete as whoever built it. Ask it for a chord somebody invented on the
spot — <Chord>Cadd9</Chord> voiced a particular way, a slash chord like <Chord>D/F#</Chord>, a
chord spelled in a notation the table's author never considered — and it either has an entry
or it does not. There is no way to ask it to figure one out.

A chord is not memorable one at a time — there are thousands of them once you count
extensions, inversions and slash bass notes — and **no table is the right size for "every chord
somebody might type."** Starting from the notes sidesteps the question of size entirely: the
method that finds a shape for <Chord>C</Chord> is the same method that finds one for a chord
that has never appeared on a chart before, because neither one was special-cased to begin with.

It is also what makes the arithmetic around a chord possible at all.
[Moving a whole sheet into another key](/tools/chord-transposer), or working out
[which fret turns the barres into open shapes](/tools/capo-calculator), both start by reading a
chord symbol as a root plus a set of intervals. **A shape cannot be transposed; a set of notes
can.**

## Why this is worth knowing as a reader, not just a builder

None of this changes what you play. **It changes what you can trust.** A diagram that is
computed from the chord's own notes is checkable against the name printed above it — if the
chord says <Chord>Am7</Chord>, the shape underneath it is answerable to A, C, E and G, and
nothing else. A diagram pulled from a table carries no such guarantee; it is only as right as
whoever typed it, for exactly the chords somebody thought to type.

The next time a chart hands you a chord you have never played, that is the question worth
asking before your fingers do anything: not "what shape is this," but "what notes is this" —
because the shape was only ever an answer to the second question, borrowed for the first.
