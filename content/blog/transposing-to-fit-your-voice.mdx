A recording sits in one key because somebody's voice sat there on the day it was recorded.
Yours might not. Transposing is the fix: move every chord in the song up or down by the same
interval, and the melody keeps its exact shape — the same distances between notes — just
starting somewhere else.

Nothing about the song changes. The words are the same, the progression is the same, the
feel is the same. Only where it sits moves.

## The key on the page is a fact, not an instruction

A chart that says `{key: G}` is not telling you to play in G. It is recording what key the
song was written or first recorded in — a fact about its history, the same kind of fact as who
wrote it. [ChordPro](/blog/chordpro-explained) even keeps it as its own line for exactly this
reason: once the original key is written down, everything else can be described *relative to
it* instead of guessed at.

That distinction is worth keeping straight, because it changes what "transpose by two" means.
It does not mean "go find the key two steps from wherever you happen to be." It means "move
two steps from the key this chart says it was written in" — the same instruction, on any song,
regardless of what key that song started in. A chart that only ever shows you the new key's
name (`now it's in A`) hides that arithmetic; one that shows the distance from the written key
(`+2`) hands it to you.

## Degrees instead of names

There is a notation built entirely around that same idea, taken further: instead of naming
each chord, it numbers it. In the key of G, <Chord>G</Chord> is written `1`, <Chord>C</Chord> is
`4`, <Chord>D</Chord> is `5` — the degree of the scale each chord starts on, not a letter at
all. It is how Nashville session players read a chart they have never seen before, from an
artist who might change key between the soundcheck and the show.

The reason it works is that a number describes a *relationship*, not a pitch. `1 4 5 1` is the
same instruction whether the song ends up in G, in D, or in Bb — nothing on the page has to
change, because nothing on the page named an absolute note to begin with. Writing a chart in
letters and one in numbers are two descriptions of the same fact, at two different distances
from the actual sound.

## The same idea, from a different control

There is a second way to change what your hands do without changing what the room hears, and
it looks like the opposite of transposing: a capo. [Capo on the second fret](/blog/capo-second-fret)
covers it in full, but the short version belongs here too, because it is easy to conflate the
two the first time you meet them — *"moving the key changes the song for everyone in the room;
moving the capo changes only what you read."* Transposing changes the sound; a capo changes
only the shape your hand holds to reach a sound that was never in question.

They answer different questions, and a chart that keeps them as two separate facts — the
song's key, and where a capo goes — lets one player choose a capo position while somebody else
plays the same progression barre, with neither of them touching the other's part.

## Why the written key still matters once you've moved

It would be simpler, on the page, to just overwrite the key every time somebody transposes and
forget what it used to be. The reason not to: the written key is the one fact a chart cannot
recover on its own once it is gone. Everything else — the new key, the capo position, which
degree each chord is — can be recomputed from it at any moment. Lose it, and a "+2" stops
meaning anything, because nobody can say two steps from *what*.

So the key that gets printed at the top of a song is worth treating as data, not decoration —
the one number every other transposition on that chart is measured against.
