Most people keep their songs in whatever was nearest: a notes app, a screenshot of somebody
else's tab, a document where the chords are held above the right syllable by a row of spaces
and a prayer. It works until the day the font changes, or the phone does, and every chord
slides half a line to the left.

ChordPro is the fix, and it is older than most of the apps that read it. It is a plain-text
format — no proprietary file, no account, no export button — in which **a chord is written in
square brackets, inline, at the exact point in the word where you change your hand.**

## What it looks like

```
{title: The Last Bus Home}
{artist: Nina Kovač}
{key: Am}
{capo: 2}

[Am]The last bus home is [F]late
and I am [C]singing any[G]way
```

That is the whole idea. The brackets sit *inside* the line, glued to the syllable they belong
to, so nothing can drift: <Chord>Am</Chord> is on "The", <Chord>F</Chord> is on "late", and
they stay there whether the line is rendered at twelve points on a phone or forty on a tablet
propped against a music stand.

The lines in curly braces are directives — metadata about the song rather than the song
itself. You will meet a handful of them constantly:

- `{title: …}` and `{artist: …}` — what the song is called and whose it is.
- `{key: …}` — the key it is written in, which is what lets an app transpose *relative* to
  something instead of guessing.
- `{capo: …}` — where the capo goes, if it goes anywhere. Separate from the key on purpose:
  [those are two different facts](/blog/capo-second-fret) and a sheet that conflates them
  makes both of them useless.
- `{comment: …}` — a note to yourself, printed alongside the words.

**Anything an app does not understand, it is expected to ignore rather than choke on.** That
is deliberate, and it is most of why the format has lasted: a file written for one program in
2011 still opens in a program written last year, minus whatever the newer one never learned.

## Where exactly the bracket goes

The one thing worth getting right on your first file is that a chord marks the syllable where
your hand moves, not the word it is nearest to. Look again at the second line above:

```
and I am [C]singing any[G]way
```

The <Chord>G</Chord> is inside "anyway", between "any" and "way", because that is where the
chord actually changes when you play it. Written as `[G]anyway` it would be a semiquaver
early; written at the end of the line it would be too late to be any use. **A chord that lands
mid-word is not a mistake in the file, it is the file being precise.**

This is also the part that makes the format tedious to type by hand and worth converting into
rather than retyping. If you already have sheets with the chords sitting above the words, a
[free converter](/tools/chordpro-converter) reads the columns and writes the brackets, and you
check the result rather than doing the work.

## Why the brackets beat the spaces

The "chords above the words" layout everybody starts with is not really a format — it is a
picture of one. It depends on the text being monospaced, on nobody wrapping a long line, and
on every future reader having the same column width you did. **Change any of the three and the
information is gone, because the information *was* the alignment.**

ChordPro stores the relationship instead of a picture of it. Once a chord is attached to a
syllable rather than to a column, an app can do things that are impossible in a screenshot:

- **Transpose.** Every chord moves by the same interval, and the words do not move at all —
  which is what [a transposer](/tools/chord-transposer) is doing when it hands you back the
  same sheet in a different key.
- **Apply a capo** and show the shapes your hand actually plays, not the ones the recording
  is in.
- **Reflow** on a narrow screen without the chords ending up over the wrong word.
- **Switch notation**, so an Italian musician reads <Chord>La</Chord> where an English one
  reads <Chord>A</Chord> — the same file, two readers.

None of that is clever. It is all downstream of one decision: write down which syllable, not
which column.

## Marking up the parts of a song

Once the chords are in, the second useful thing a ChordPro file can carry is structure. A
chorus is wrapped in a pair of directives, and so is a block of tablature:

```
{start_of_chorus}
[F]Sing it again, and [C]again
{end_of_chorus}

{start_of_tab}
e|---0---2---3---|
B|---1---3---0---|
{end_of_tab}
```

Both pairs have short forms — `{soc}` and `{eoc}`, `{sot}` and `{eot}` — and both mean the
same thing to a reader as to a program: this block is different from the verse around it. The
tab pair matters more than it looks. **A tab block tells the app to leave the rows alone**:
not to reflow them, not to read the letters at the start of each line as chords, not to touch
the alignment that is the entire content of a tablature row.

## What ChordPro does not do

It is a lyric-and-chord format, and being honest about the edges is the fastest way to know
whether it fits your repertoire:

- **It does not carry rhythm.** There is no way to say that the <Chord>F</Chord> lasts two
  bars and the <Chord>C</Chord> lasts one. You get the order and the placement, not the
  duration.
- **It does not carry a melody.** If you need notes on a stave, ChordPro is the wrong file
  and you want notation software.
- **It does not carry audio.** Backing tracks, click tracks and recordings live outside it,
  and apps that support them do it with their own extensions.
- **Apps disagree about the rarer directives.** `{a: …}` is the artist in one program and the
  *album* in another; `{st: …}` is a subtitle in the specification and was redefined by a
  popular app to mean the artist. The core — brackets, `{title}`, `{key}`, choruses — is
  universal; the edges are dialect. Anything importing a large library should be sniffing
  which program wrote a file before trusting its metadata.

## It outlives the app

The quiet argument for ChordPro is not any feature. It is that **the file is readable by a
human being with a text editor, twenty years from now, on a device nobody has designed yet.**
A repertoire is a thing you build slowly and expect to keep; storing it in a format only one
company can open is how people lose a decade of work when that company is bought.

The files themselves are ordinary text with an unglamorous set of extensions — `.chopro`,
`.cho`, `.crd`, `.pro` — and any of them opens in any editor on any machine. That is not a
low bar cleared by accident. It is the bar.

If your songs are already in the spaces-and-prayer layout, they are not lost — the conversion
is mechanical, and a decent importer will do it for you and show you the result before it
saves anything, because the conversion is a heuristic and it will not always guess right. The
[longer piece on converting](/blog/convert-chord-sheets-to-chordpro) covers where it goes
wrong and how to spot it.

What matters is where they end up: in a format that will still be a format when the app has
changed hands, or names, or gone.
