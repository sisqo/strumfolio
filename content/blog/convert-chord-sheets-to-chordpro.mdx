If you have collected chord sheets for any length of time, you have a folder of text files that
all look the same: a line of chord names, then the words underneath, the two held together by a
row of spaces. It is the oldest format on the internet and it works perfectly until the moment
anything about the display changes.

Converting that to ChordPro takes one paste. What follows is what the conversion is doing, so
you can tell when it has got it wrong.

## Convert a chord sheet in four steps

1. **Copy the whole sheet**, chord lines and lyric lines together. The blank lines between
   verses can come too — they mark the sections and survive the conversion.
2. **Paste it into the [free ChordPro converter](/tools/chordpro-converter).** It runs in your
   browser: nothing is uploaded, nothing is stored, and there is nothing to sign up for.
3. **Read the output**, which appears as you paste. The check takes five seconds and is
   described below.
4. Copy it back out, or download it as a `.chopro` file. That file is plain text and yours.

That is genuinely the whole procedure. The rest of this article is about the part nobody tells
you: **the conversion is a guess**, and you should know which guess it is making.

## What changes, exactly

Here is a sheet in the format almost everything is published in:

```
Am                   F
The last bus home is late
    C            G
and I am singing anyway
```

And the same thing as ChordPro:

```
[Am]The last bus home is [F]late
and [C]I am singing [G]anyway
```

The chord names have not changed and the words have not changed. **What changed is what the
chord is attached to.** In the first version <Chord>F</Chord> is at column 21 and happens to
have "late" underneath it. In the second it is *in* the line, immediately before the word it
belongs to.

That is the whole idea, and every useful thing follows from it. A chord tied to a syllable can
be transposed, capo'd, reflowed on a phone, or reprinted at any size, and it stays over the
word it was always over. A chord tied to column 21 survives none of those — change the font,
open it on a narrower screen, let one long line wrap, and every chord slides away from the
word it belonged to.

## The guess the converter is making

To convert a sheet, something has to decide which lines are chords and which are words. There
is no marker — both are just text.

The rule is: **a line is a chord line if every token on it parses as a chord name.** That gets
it right nearly always, and *nearly* is the interesting part. A line reading <Chord>A</Chord>
on its own could be a chord or the first word of a sentence. A line of `Do Re Mi` is three
chords in Italian notation and three syllables in English.

So the conversion is a heuristic. It is a good one, and it will still be wrong on some sources
— which is why the converter shows you the output instead of handing you a file, and why the
import screen inside the app keeps the converted song editable before it saves anything.

The bias is deliberate and worth knowing, because it decides which way the mistakes fall: a
chord line mistaken for lyrics leaves a stray line you can see and fix, while **lyrics mistaken
for chords take words out of the song** and hide them above an unrelated line. Given the
choice, the converter takes the first.

## How to check it in five seconds

Read the output and look at where the brackets landed. You are checking one thing: **is each
chord immediately before the syllable you actually change hands on?**

The failure has a signature. A misaligned source produces brackets inside words —
`hom[F]e`, `sing[C]ing` — because the chord's column fell in the middle of a word rather than
at its start:

```
Am                  F
The last bus home is late
```

```
[Am]The last bus home i[F]s late
```

One character of drift in the input is one misplaced bracket in the output. **The fix is in
the input, not the output**: nudge the chord line left or right until the chord sits over the
first letter of the word, and paste again. Fighting the result instead means fixing the same
thing once per verse.

## Things that are not chord sheets

A few kinds of file look close enough to confuse a converter and are worth knowing about:

- **Tab.** Six lines of `e|---3---0---` are not chords above words, and converting them as if
  they were produces nonsense. A good converter leaves tab rows alone; this one does.
- **Lyrics with no chords at all.** Nothing to convert — the words come through unchanged,
  which is the right answer rather than an error.
- **Something already in ChordPro.** Also nothing to do. The converter says so instead of
  bracketing the brackets.
- **A chord chart with bar lines**, `| Am | F | C |`. The names convert; the bars are not
  lyrics, so what you get back is the chords with nothing underneath them, which is what that
  chart always was.

## More than one song in the paste

ChordPro marks a song's start with a `{title: …}` directive, which means a file holding a
whole songbook can be split on those boundaries without guessing. If you paste several songs
at once, the converter will say how many it found, and importing the same paste into
Strumfolio splits them into separate songs rather than one very long one.

## What to do with the file afterwards

A converted sheet is a `.chopro` file, and it is worth treating as a document rather than a
clipboard step:

- **Keep it somewhere you back up.** It is plain text — a folder in a cloud drive, a git repo,
  an email to yourself. Any of those outlives any app.
- **Name it after the song, not the source.** `the-last-bus-home.chopro` beats
  `ug-tab-copy-3.txt` the first time you look for it in a hurry.
- **Open it in something that reads ChordPro** to see the chords land over the right
  syllables at any size. That is the point of the conversion, and it is only visible once
  something renders it.
- **Transpose it after converting, not before.** ChordPro survives a key change exactly; the
  column layout barely does — the [chord transposer](/tools/chord-transposer) will move a
  whole sheet either way and tell you when a column could not be kept.

## A note for people leaving another app

If your sheets came out of OnSong, MobileSheets or SongbookPro, they may already be ChordPro —
but in that app's own dialect, where the same directive means different things. `{a: …}` is
the artist in OnSong and the *album* in MobileSheets. `{st: …}` is a subtitle in the
specification and was redefined by OnSong to mean the artist.

Read with the wrong table, each of those puts a correct value under the wrong heading,
silently. **A wrong value that arrived quietly is worse than a missing one**, because nothing
ever prompts you to look at it again. It is worth checking that whatever you import with knows
the difference — [Strumfolio's importer does](/blog/onsong-alternative), and refuses to guess
when nothing identifies the source.

## Why bother at all

Because the file outlives the app. A `.chopro` file is plain text: it opens in any editor, it
goes into version control, and it will still be readable when the app you typed it in has
changed hands or names or gone. That argument is worth its own article, and it has
[one](/blog/chordpro-explained).
