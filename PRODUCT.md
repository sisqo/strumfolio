# Product

## Users

Musicians — solo players and bands — reading their own repertoire of lyrics and chords
while performing or rehearsing, on a tablet or phone propped up in front of them. The
context is physical: hands are on an instrument, eyes glance down between phrases, light
ranges from a lit rehearsal room to a dark stage. Registration is open to anyone with an
email address; each account is its own private repertoire, with a small set of global
owners who administer the whole installation. There is no shared or public repertoire to
design for, only each reader's own.

Three other people meet the product without ever owning a repertoire, and each gets a
screen of their own rather than a cut-down reader:

- **The guest** — somebody handed a Strum Together link, who follows the leader's song and
  key from a page that asks for no account, no install, and nothing beyond a theme and an
  instrument.
- **The visitor** — somebody deciding whether to sign up, met by the landing page, the
  pricing page, a blog and six free tools that do their whole job in the browser. Those
  pages are the only marketing there is.
- **The operator** — a global owner running the installation from inside the app:
  accounts, plans, campaigns, where sign-ups came from. Every operator screen is built to
  be believed before it is built to be pretty.

## Product Purpose

Keep the text readable and the hands free while playing. Every reading control — zoom,
auto-scroll, key transposition, notation switch, capo, metronome — exists because it serves
that one job, and each is a thumb's reach away rather than buried in a settings menu. Songs
are organized into songbooks (canzonieri), each a folder for a set or a repertoire; an edit
is on the reading screen the moment it is saved, on every device the reader owns, and the
whole repertoire is kept on each device so that no connection is needed to open it. Success
looks like: a player never loses their place, on any device, with or without a connection.

## What It Does

**A repertoire, organized.** Songs live inside songbooks — one set list, one band, one
occasion each — and a songbook splits into sections so a long repertoire stays a set of
short lists rather than one long scroll; a section folds closed with a tap and stays closed
for the evening, not for ever. Reordering, in both songbooks and sections, is a drag with a
finger or a keyboard, on stage as much as at a desk; search reaches every song at once,
because the first question when looking for a song is rarely which songbook it's in. A star
marks the songs that matter tonight, and one switch shows only those — on the home screen,
inside a songbook, and still on when the reader comes back from a song for the next one.
From the reading screen two arrows reach the previous and the next song in the songbook, so
a set is played through without going back to the list.

**Reading, tuned for the stage.** Chords sit above the words they belong to, the way
printed music does. What the song *is* — its written key, its capo, its tempo — is stated in
chips under the title, and what this reader does to it is kept apart from that: a semitone
stepper transposes on a tap, always shown as the distance from the song's own written key
rather than forcing anyone to think in absolute note names; a capo suggestion finds the
fret that leaves the most chords open, so the hand plays the easiest shape, not just the
right sound. Every chord is also a button — tap it and see the fingering, worked out from
the notes that make the chord rather than looked up in an external library, with the
alternate shapes a swipe away and the one chosen remembered for that song. The sheet draws
chords four ways — names only, fingerings or diagrams once above the song, or a diagram on
the very word — for guitar on every plan and ukulele on the paid ones. Auto-scroll runs at
one of eight speeds with the screen held awake for it, and a manual scroll pauses it rather
than fighting it. A metronome lives in the same bar, at a tempo remembered per song and
taken from the song's own directive until the reader says otherwise; it flashes as well as
clicks, so it is still a metronome on a phone carried on silent. Tablature blocks stay
folded under a placeholder until asked for. Four notations — Italian, international, German
with H for B, and Nashville numbers, which read the same however far the song is
transposed — switch per reader, never per song: they are how a sheet is drawn, not what is
stored in it, so an export reads the same for anybody.

**Notes on the word.** A reader can pin a private note to the exact syllable, or to the
chord standing over it — a fingering, a cue, the line they always get wrong. Notes belong to
the reader and not to the song, are gated by no plan, are there with no signal and are kept
if written with none. Hiding them is a gesture for the evening rather than a preference, so
the page comes back clean for a gig and the notes come back afterwards.

**Bringing songs in, and keeping them current.** There is no catalogue to browse — every
song is one somebody brought in, except the one songbook of public-domain traditionals a
new account is created with, so the first screen has something playable on it instead of
being empty. That songbook is ordinary from the moment it exists: editable, renamable,
deletable, and counted against the plan like any other. The importer takes fifteen kinds of
file — ChordPro and the dialects other apps write, OnSong, MusicXML, a SongbookPro backup,
PDF, Word, and a zip of any of those, whose folders become sections — and any text pasted
in, converting "chords above the lyrics" copied from anywhere else. Everything is parsed in
the browser, with no AI anywhere, and always behind a preview that stays editable before it
saves, because the conversion is a heuristic and won't always get it right: a PDF is split
into songs by its titles or, failing those, by its pages, and a scan is refused in words
rather than shown as an empty song. Pasting more than one song at once splits automatically
on the boundaries ChordPro itself defines. A song already added is never finished: the
visual editor keeps a chord pinned to the syllable it belongs to while the lyrics are edited
in place — split a line, join two, drag a chord to a different beat, seat one past the last
word where the turnaround goes — without ever hand-writing a bracket, and the source view is
one tap away for whoever prefers the brackets, the two never disagreeing. Every edit is on
the reading screen the moment it's saved, and on every other device as soon as it is online.

**Taking songs out.** Underneath every song is standard ChordPro, and nothing here is kept
in a format only Strumfolio understands. Export hands it back: one song, or the whole
repertoire as a zip — a plain backup, or a copy organized into folders for reading
elsewhere. The booklet turns one songbook into a typeset PDF, one song a page, with a cover
and an index, ready to print and hand out. It prints each song in its *written* key by
default, because a booklet is for a room where the reader's own capo means nothing; the
reader can ask for their own key and capo instead, one download at a time, and every song
printed that way says so on its own page.

**Never without the repertoire.** Installed as a PWA, Strumfolio opens instantly with no
connection. Once a reader is signed in, their own repertoire is warmed into the device's
cache in the background, one page at a time rather than in a burst, so a song they have
never opened on this phone is still there when the signal is not. A rehearsal room with no
signal, or a stage with a phone in airplane mode, changes nothing about reading, notes or
the metronome. Strum Together is the one thing that needs the connection, because keeping a
room in step is what it is.

**Playing as more than one.** Strum Together turns a solo reading session into a shared
one: whoever's playing shares a link, and anyone who opens it — no account, no install —
follows the same song in the same key from their own screen, automatically, whether it's a
duo or a full room singing along. The leader's key and notation travel with the song; the
guest keeps their own theme, instrument and chord shapes, because those are about the hand
holding the screen. Every plan can lead a session, the free one included; how many screens
may follow is what the plans change, and the leader's own device is never counted.

**A private space, not a library to browse.** Every account is one person's own, created by
signing in with Google or with an email and password — nobody invited, nobody excluded, and
no collaborators: an address and an account are the same thing. There is no advertising and
no third-party tracking; the one cookie that is not strictly necessary records which
campaign brought a sign-up, and the policies say so. The free plan holds a real repertoire
with no time limit, Strum Together included — a session with one other screen following;
paid plans lift the songbook and song caps, bring more of the room into a session, and add
what makes sense to gate — ukulele shapes, a printed booklet, asking for a feature — never
reading, editing, notes, notation, leading a session, or playing what's already there.
Subscriptions run monthly or yearly and Lifetime is one payment; cancelling stops the next
renewal and keeps the period already paid for, an upgrade is immediate and a downgrade
waits, and fourteen days buy a full refund with no reason asked. A promotional link carries
a percentage off for a stated stretch and is shown only to whoever arrived with it. Leaving
is a button in the reader's own settings, not a request to send.

## The public side

Everything a stranger can reach is written to say what the product is and to be found by
whoever is looking for one. The landing page shows the reading screen before it says
anything, because the product is a page of words and chords and no sentence beats looking at
one; the pricing page puts the plans side by side and reads every number from the same table
the app enforces. A blog, «Playing notes», carries guides on capo, keys and chord shapes,
and comparisons of the apps a musician might be choosing between. Six free tools do their
whole job in the browser with no account — convert a sheet to ChordPro, transpose it, find
the capo, time a set, and look up any guitar or ukulele chord from the same shape engine the
app uses — each a page a search engine can index and a visitor can use once and leave. A
public changelog records the releases worth telling a reader about, never the commits. The
legal pages are written for the reader rather than against them, and they are kept true: a
change to what the app stores or sends is a change to them.

## Operating it

The global owners run the installation from inside the app, and every operator screen holds
to one rule: a read that failed shows a dash, never a reassuring value, because «nothing is
wrong» and «could not tell» are opposite answers on a screen built to be believed. The
accounts surface states the plan in force and the gift queued behind it before it shows the
facts; from it an operator can give a plan by hand and offer to tell the reader so, suspend
an account, reset a password, or open an account for an address that will never find the
registration form. A leads page says which campaign brought each sign-up, and says out loud
how many arrived with no attribution rather than letting a total drop them. Campaigns are
made and read there, every email the app sends can be previewed and test-sent, and each of
the notifications an operator gets on their phone — a registration, a purchase, a
cancellation, a piece of feedback — can be switched off. A reader's newsletter choice is the
consent every marketing message has to pass, and an unreadable choice refuses. Nothing runs
on a schedule: every message a reader receives, and every change to what they are entitled
to, is the direct consequence of something a person did a moment earlier. Readers reach the
operator through the app's own feedback sheet, open from everywhere except the reading
screen and the editor.

## Brand Personality

Precise, warm, considered. The app already speaks this through code, not just visuals: two
themes each hand-tuned rather than one inverted from the other, an accent color reserved
first for chords so nothing else competes with the one thing that must stay unmistakable,
and a corner-radius scale built from what each shape's job is rather than an arbitrary
size. Nothing decorative ships without a stated reason. Warmth comes from the neutral
palette and the unhurried, well-explained craft — never from mascots, playfulness, or
marketing flourish.

## Anti-references

Ad-heavy, cluttered chord/tab sites (Ultimate Guitar and similar) — anything that trades
distraction-free reading for ad slots, popups, or a busy page. Also avoid generic
dark-developer-tool aesthetics (flat near-black surfaces with a single neon accent) —
Strumfolio's dark theme is warm and hand-tuned, not a default inversion.

## Design Principles

- **One job, done well.** Every control on the reading screen earns its place by serving
  legibility or hands-free playing; nothing is added because a settings menu had room.
- **Both themes are designed, not inverted.** Light and dark are each their own considered
  surface — elevation, accent, and shadow are rethought per theme, not swapped by formula.
- **The accent is the chord's color first.** Anything else that borrows the accent (a
  badge, an active control) stays deliberately quieter than the chords themselves.
- **What you save, you see.** An edit is on the reading screen the moment it is saved, and
  on every other device the reader owns as soon as it is online — the reader is never
  staring at a stale sheet while a correction sits somewhere else.
- **Offline is a real mode, not a fallback.** The repertoire is put on the device before it
  is needed, and the service worker is designed for, not patched in after the fact — a song
  already saved must keep working with no connection.
- **The song is the song; the reading is the reader's.** Key, capo and tempo as written
  belong to the song and are stated on it. Transposition, notation, instrument, chord
  shapes, the reader's own tempo, notes and stars belong to the reader, follow them across
  every song and device, and never change a file — so an export reads the same for anybody.
- **Copy from the code.** Every count and every plan claim on a public page reads from the
  constant the app enforces, so the page cannot outlive the rule. What is said is said on
  the positive and absences stay unnamed; a mechanism is named only when the build has it,
  so no payment processor is mentioned while nobody is charged.
- **Nothing happens to a reader that a person did not just do.** No scheduled job and no
  drip: an inbox and an entitlement change only as the direct consequence of an action a
  moment earlier, and anything the platform ever aims at a reader is built to happen at
  most once.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

No formal WCAG target beyond the current considered-by-default bar: reduced-motion
alternatives on every animation, visible focus rings, and contrast checked across both
hand-tuned themes rather than assumed from one. Where a sense may be missing on stage, the
other carries the signal — the metronome flashes as well as clicks. Keep that bar on any new
surface; raise a flag if a specific WCAG level or user need comes up later.
